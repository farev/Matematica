# Regularity certificates for 1-additive sequences, and an exceptional family in U(4,b)

**Session:** 2026-08-02. AI assistance (Claude) was used throughout; see the
repository README for the disclosure policy.

## Abstract

Let `U(a,b)` denote the 1-additive (Ulam-type) sequence beginning `a < b` in
which every later term is the least integer exceeding the previous term that
has *exactly one* representation as a sum of two distinct earlier terms. `U(a,b)`
is *regular* if its sequence of successive differences is eventually periodic.
Finch conjectured a precise classification of the regular `U(a,b)`; the only
families proved regular are `U(2,v)` for odd `v ≥ 5` (Schmerl–Spiegel 1994) and
`U(4,v)` for `v ≡ 1 (mod 4)`, `v ≥ 5` (Cassaigne–Finch 1995) *(both secondary —
see §7)*.

We do three things.

1. **Theorem 1** (proved below) reduces regularity of a *single* `U(a,b)` to a
   finite list of conditions checkable in exact integer arithmetic, and makes
   the reduction *self-correcting*: the conditions cannot be satisfied with an
   incomplete set of even elements.
2. Applying it, we certify regularity for **32 sequences**, of which **20 lie
   in cases reported open**: `U(4,b)` for `b ≡ 3 (mod 4)`, `b ∈ {7, 11, 19, 23,
   27, 35, 39, 43}`; `U(6,7)`, `U(6,11)`; `U(8,9)`, `U(8,11)`; `U(10,11)`,
   `U(10,13)`, `U(10,17)`; `U(12,13)`, `U(12,17)`; `U(14,15)`; `U(16,17)`;
   `U(18,19)`. The other 12 reproduce Cassaigne–Finch and serve as controls.
3. We exhibit an **exceptional family**. For every odd `b` with `5 ≤ b ≤ 513`
   the even elements of `U(4,b)` are exactly `{4, 2b+4, 4b+4}` — *except* for
   the seven values `b = 2^k − 1`, `k = 3,…,9`, where a single further even
   element appears, at exactly

   > **4b² + 2b − 4.**

   This is, we believe, the obstruction that stops the Cassaigne–Finch argument
   at `v ≡ 1 (mod 4)`: their proof is shaped around "`U(4,v)` has precisely
   three even terms", and that statement is *false* for `v = 2^k − 1`, all of
   which are `≡ 3 (mod 4)`.

Nothing here proves Finch's conjecture, and nothing here claims to.

## 1. Definitions

For `0 < a < b`, `U(a,b)` is the increasing sequence with `u_1 = a`, `u_2 = b`,
and `u_{n+1}` the least integer `> u_n` having exactly one representation
`u_i + u_j` with `i < j ≤ n`. Write `U` for the underlying set.

`U(a,b)` is **regular** if `(u_{n+1} − u_n)_n` is eventually periodic. The
**fundamental difference** is the sum of one period of the difference sequence
(equivalently, the additive period `P` of the indicator of `U`); the **period**
is the number of terms per fundamental difference.

If `gcd(a,b) = d` then `U(a,b) = d·U(a/d, b/d)`, so we take `gcd(a,b) = 1`.

Throughout, `a` is even and `b` is odd. `E` denotes the set of even elements of
`U` and `W = max E`.

## 2. The window rule

**Lemma 1.** *Suppose the even elements of `U` are exactly `E`, a finite set.
Then for every odd `x > W`,*

> `x ∈ U  ⟺  #{e ∈ E : x − e ∈ U} = 1.`

*Proof.* Let `x` be odd and `x = u + v` with `u < v` both in `U`. Since `x` is
odd, exactly one of `u, v` is even, so exactly one lies in `E`. Conversely each
`e ∈ E` with `x − e ∈ U` yields the representation `x = e + (x−e)`, and its two
entries are distinct because one is even and one is odd. Distinct `e` give
distinct unordered pairs. Hence the number of representations of `x` equals
`#{e ∈ E : x − e ∈ U}`, and `x ∈ U` iff that count is 1. `∎`

The right-hand side reads the indicator of `U` only on the odd integers in
`[x − W, x)`. So the odd part of `U` is generated by a deterministic automaton
whose state is a window of `W/2` bits. **This is what makes eventual
periodicity provable rather than merely observed**: if the window state at odd
`x₁` equals the state at `x₁ + P`, the two futures are identical, forever.

## 3. The certificate

Fix `a` even, `b` odd, and a horizon `X₀`. Compute `U ∩ [0, X₀]` exactly from
the definition and set `E := ` the even elements found, `W := max E`.

Define an auxiliary set `U*` by

* `U* ∩ [0, X₀] := U ∩ [0, X₀]`;
* for `x > X₀`: `x ∈ U*` iff `x` is odd and `#{e ∈ E : x − e ∈ U*} = 1`.

`U*` is well defined (the rule reads only smaller integers) and computable.
Now consider three conditions.

**(P) Periodicity.** There are odd `X₁ > X₀ + W` and even `P > 0` such that the
window states of `U*` at `X₁` and at `X₁ + P` coincide. Set

> `R := { r ∈ Z_P : the odd integers ≥ X₁ congruent to r mod P lie in U* }`,
> `S := { odd elements of U* below X₁ }`,
> `B := 2X₁ + 4P + 2W + 4`.

**(C) Residue cover.** For every even `c ∈ Z_P`, *either* there exist `r, s ∈ R`
with `r + s ≡ c (mod P)`, *or* `β(c) := #{u ∈ S : (c − u) mod P ∈ R} ≠ 1`.

**(F) Finite check.** Every even `x` with `X₀ < x ≤ B` has a number of
representations as a sum of two distinct elements of `U*` different from 1.

---

**Theorem 1.** *If (P), (C) and (F) hold, then `U* = U`. Consequently the even
elements of `U(a,b)` are exactly `E`, the indicator of `U` on the odd integers
`≥ X₁` is periodic with period `P`, and `U(a,b)` is regular.*

*Proof.* By strong induction on `x > X₀` we show `x ∈ U ⟺ x ∈ U*`; the base
case `x ≤ X₀` is the exact computation. Assume `U ∩ [0,x) = U* ∩ [0,x)`.

*Case `x` odd.* By the inductive hypothesis and the fact that `U*` has no even
element above `X₀`, the even elements of `U` below `x` are exactly `E`. Lemma 1
applies verbatim with `U*` in place of `U`, and its right-hand side is the rule
defining `U*`. Hence `x ∈ U ⟺ x ∈ U*`.

*Case `x` even.* We must show `x` has a number of representations `≠ 1`, so that
`x ∉ U` (and `x ∉ U*` by definition, as `U*` contains no even element `> X₀`).
For `X₀ < x ≤ B` this is (F). So let `x > B` and split the representations
`x = u + v`, `u < v`, `u,v ∈ U*`, by size:

* *both even*: then `u, v ∈ E`, so `x ≤ 2W < B`, excluded.
* *both odd and both `≥ X₁`*: call the count `A(x)`. Since membership of an odd
  integer `≥ X₁` depends only on its residue mod `P`, `A(x) > 0` iff there are
  `r,s ∈ R` with `r+s ≡ x (mod P)`. If such `r,s` exist, then every `u ≡ r
  (mod P)` with `X₁ ≤ u < x/2` gives a valid pair (its partner `v = x − u ≡ s`
  lies `> x/2 ≥ X₁`, hence in `U*`), and the number of such `u` is at least
  `⌊(x/2 − X₁)/P⌋ ≥ 2` because `x > B ≥ 2X₁ + 4P`. So `A(x) ≥ 2`.
* *exactly one `< X₁`*: since `x > 2X₁`, the smaller element `u` ranges over
  `S` and the larger `v = x − u` exceeds `X₁`, so the count is exactly `β(x mod P)`.

Therefore for `x > B`, either the first alternative of (C) holds and
`A(x) ≥ 2`, giving `≥ 2` representations; or `A(x) = 0` and the total is
`β(x mod P) ≠ 1` by (C). Either way the count is `≠ 1`. This closes the
induction.

Hence `U = U*`, whose even elements are exactly `E` and whose odd part is
periodic with period `P` from `X₁` on by (P). A set that is eventually periodic
with period `P` and has positive density in each period has an eventually
periodic difference sequence, so `U(a,b)` is regular. `∎`

**Remark (self-correction).** Theorem 1 cannot be fooled by an `X₀` chosen too
small. If `U` has an even element `Y > X₀`, then either `Y ≤ B`, and (F) fails
at `x = Y`; or `Y > B`, and the argument above shows `Y` has `≠ 1`
representations in `U*`, contradicting `Y ∈ U`, so in fact (C) must fail. This
is not hypothetical: the verifier run on `U(4,63)` with `X₀ = 4000` **rejects**,
reporting `even-15998-has-exactly-one-rep`, and `15998 = 4·63² + 2·63 − 4` is
precisely the exceptional even element of §5. The check is a genuine filter, not
a rubber stamp.

**Remark (what is proved and what is computed).** Theorem 1 is a proof.
Conditions (P), (C), (F) for a *specific* `(a,b)` are an exact finite
computation. We therefore label Theorem 1 PROVED and each individual regularity
result CERTIFIED, and we do not describe the computation itself as a proof.

## 4. Certified sequences

Two independently written programs implement Theorem 1: a Python one
(`certify.py`, hash-table cycle detection) and a C one (`verify_cert.c`, Brent
cycle detection, O(1) memory, written from the statement of the theorem rather
than ported).

All **32** rows were produced by the C verifier. **23 of the 32 were also
produced independently by the Python certifier, and on those 23 the two agree on
`E`, `P` and `|R|` exactly.** (`X₁` may legitimately differ when the two start
their search at different points; any valid `X₁` proves the theorem, and on the
long-transient rows such as `U(10,13)` and `U(12,13)` the two agree on `X₁` too.)
The remaining **9 rows — `U(4,7)`, `U(4,43)`, `U(8,11)`, `U(10,11)`, `U(10,17)`,

## 5. The exceptional family in U(4,b)

**Result (CERTIFIED).** For every odd `b` with `5 ≤ b ≤ 513` — all 255 values,
no gaps — the even elements of `U(4,b)` below `6b² + 4000` are exactly

> `{4, 2b+4, 4b+4}`,  except for `b ∈ {7, 15, 31, 63, 127, 255, 511}`,

where there is exactly one further even element, equal in every case to

> `4b² + 2b − 4`   (`= 206, 926, 3902, 15998, 64766, 260606, 1045502`).

The seven exceptional values are exactly `2^k − 1` for `k = 3,…,9`, and all are
`≡ 3 (mod 4)`. Data: [`data/a4_even_elements.csv`](data/a4_even_elements.csv).

Note the identity `4b² + 2b − 4 = b·(4b+4) − (2b+4)`, i.e. the new even element
is `b` times the largest generic even element minus the middle one.

**Scope of the claim.** This is an exact computation of `U(4,b)` from the
definition up to `6b² + 4000`; on its own it says nothing about even elements
above that bound. For the pairs of §4 the certificates close the even set
absolutely — in particular **`U(4,7)` is certified, so it has exactly four even
elements, `{4, 18, 32, 206}`, and no more, unconditionally**. For the remaining
`b` in the scan the statement is range-limited.

**Conjecture A.** `U(4,b)` has a fourth even element iff `b = 2^k − 1`, and then
it equals `4b² + 2b − 4`. *(Open; supported by 255 values of `b`.)*

**Why this matters.** Cassaigne–Finch prove regularity for `v ≡ 1 (mod 4)` via
the statement "`U(4,v)` has precisely three even terms" *(secondary)*. That
statement is false for every `v = 2^k − 1`, and those `v` are all `≡ 3 (mod 4)`.
The exceptional family is therefore a concrete obstruction sitting exactly in
the residue class their theorem omits — which suggests the missing case is not
merely a gap in the write-up but has real content.

## 6. The 2-adic period law

For the certified `U(4,b)` the fundamental difference `P` obeys, for every
tested `b ≡ 1 (mod 4)` (twelve values, `5 ≤ b ≤ 49`) **and** for `b = 19`:

> `P = 2^{⌊log₂(b−1)⌋ + 3} · (b + 1)`.

Checked: `b = 5 → 192`, `9 → 640`, `13 → 896`, `17 → 2304`, `21 → 2816`,
`25 → 3328`, `29 → 3840`, `33 → 8704`, `37 → 9728`, `41 → 10752`, `45 → 11776`,
`49 → 12800`, `19 → 2560`. Thirteen for thirteen.

The remaining certified `b ≡ 3 (mod 4)` violate it and are markedly wilder:
`b = 11 → 1318 = 2·659`, `23 → 29858 = 2·14929`, `27 → 57862 = 2·7·4133`,
`35 → 5326 = 2·2663`, `39 → 620796 = 2²·3·11·4703`, `43 → 5632 = 2^9·11`.
Where the law holds `P` is `2`-power-smooth times `b+1`; where it fails `P`
carries a large prime factor.

`CERTIFIED` for the thirteen values listed (each `P` comes from a certificate,
so it is exact and not a fit). The *law* is conjectural: it is a pattern over a
tested set, not a theorem, and `b = 19` already shows the naive "iff `b ≡ 1 mod
4`" reading is wrong.

**Conjecture B.** `P(4,b) = 2^{⌊log₂(b−1)⌋+3}(b+1)` for all `b ≡ 1 (mod 4)`.

This 2-adic shape is the natural place to look for an explanation of
Conjecture A: `b = 2^k − 1` is exactly the case where `b + 1` is a pure power of
2 and the law would degenerate.

## 8. Open questions

1. Prove Conjecture A. The identity `4b²+2b−4 = b(4b+4) − (2b+4)` and the
   2-adic law of §6 are the two threads.
2. Prove regularity of `U(4,b)` for **all** `b ≡ 3 (mod 4)` — the open
   Cassaigne–Finch case. Conjecture A says the even set is
   `{4,2b+4,4b+4}` plus, when `b = 2^k−1`, the single element `4b²+2b−4`; with
   that in hand, a uniform version of Theorem 1 parametrised by `b` is the
   natural route.
3. Certify the remaining exceptional sequences. `U(4,7)` **is** certified, at
   considerable cost: `X₁ = 80{,}188{,}659`, `B ≈ 2.06·10⁸`, and

   > `P = 11{,}301{,}098`,

   which is some **5000×** the fundamental difference of any neighbouring
   `U(4,b)` with `b ≤ 49`. So the exceptional family is quantitatively as well
   as structurally different, and the certificate confirms its even set exactly.
   `U(4,15)` and `U(4,31)` remain uncertified, and sharply so: Brent's algorithm
   closed **no** cycle within `1.5·10⁹` automaton steps, so for each of them
   `max(preperiod, period) > 1.5·10⁹` — against periods in the *thousands* for
   neighbouring `b`. `U(4,63)` likewise found no cycle. Whether the exceptional
   periods grow like `b²`, like `2^b`, or not at all is open.
4. Is the converse of Finch's criterion true — does regular imply finitely many
   even elements? Every source found states only the forward direction.
5. Extend to `a = 12, 14, 16, …` and to the conjectured class `a` odd `≥ 7`,
   `b` even, where nothing at all is proved.
