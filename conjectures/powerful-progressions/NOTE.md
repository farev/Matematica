# Consecutive powerful numbers in arithmetic progression: a certified census to 10^19

**Session:** 2026-08-05 · **Status of every claim labelled** PROVED / CERTIFIED / NUMERICAL.
*This note was produced with substantial AI assistance (Claude); all proofs
below are elementary and short enough to check by hand, and every computational
claim ships code and data in this directory.*

## Abstract

A *powerful* number is a positive integer `n` such that `p² | n` for every
prime `p | n`. Van Doorn (arXiv:2605.06697, May 2026) **(secondary)**
conjectured that infinitely many three-term arithmetic progressions consist of
three *consecutive* terms of the powerful-number sequence — which would answer
a question of Erdős in the negative — and reported the complete list of such
triples below 10^14: there are 18, the smallest being (1728, 1764, 1800).

We extend the census five orders of magnitude, to 10^19: there are exactly
**346** such triples, and they collapse, under componentwise rational scaling,
to **16 primitive triples**. Each scaling class has a canonical gcd-1 **root**
— an integer AP that need not itself be powerful — and we prove the exact
criterion (Lemma 1′) for which multiples of a root are componentwise powerful.
In root coordinates the census acquires structure invisible in the raw list:
the class of (1728, 1764, 1800) is generated by **(48, 49, 50)**, one root has
common difference **7** at height 2.8×10^7, and one class contains consecutive
triples at non-integer ratio 45/2. Beyond the published horizon the census
shows: a primitive triple none of whose elements is a perfect square (the
other fifteen contain exactly one), three primitives with odd common
difference, no chain saturated for long (the dominant chain, 272 of the 346
triples, first loses a multiple at m = 288), and no four-term progression of
consecutive powerful numbers below 10^19. Every triple is verified by an
independent implementation; the sweep reproduces the published powerful-number
counts at 10^10–10^12 and van Doorn's 18 at 10^14.

## 1. Definitions

- `n` is **powerful** if `min_p v_p(n) ≥ 2` over primes `p | n`; equivalently
  `n = a²b³` with `b` squarefree, and this representation is unique.
- Write the powerful numbers in increasing order as `q_1 = 1 < q_2 = 4 < …`.
  A **(consecutive) triple** is `(q_i, q_{i+1}, q_{i+2})` with
  `q_{i+1} − q_i = q_{i+2} − q_{i+1} = d`.
- Two triples are **equivalent** if one is a componentwise rational multiple
  of the other. Within the census below `X`, the smallest member of a class is
  its **primitive**; the class is its **chain**.
- The **root** of a class is `T₀ = T / gcd(T)` for any member `T` (independent
  of the member chosen). `T₀` is an integer AP with coprime-as-a-tuple
  entries, in general **not** powerful; every member of the class is `m·T₀`
  for an integer `m` (a rational `c = p/q` with `q > 1` cannot map a gcd-1
  integer tuple to an integer tuple).
- The **conjecture** (van Doorn, arXiv:2605.06697 **(secondary)**): there are
  infinitely many consecutive triples.

## 2. Lemmas

**Lemma 1 (admissible multipliers of a powerful triple). PROVED.**
Let `T = (x, y, z)` be a triple of powerful numbers and `k ≥ 1`. Then
`kx, ky, kz` are all powerful **iff** for every prime `p | k`,
`v_p(k) + min(v_p(x), v_p(y), v_p(z)) ≥ 2`.

*Proof.* For `w ∈ {x, y, z}`, `kw` is powerful iff `v_p(kw) ≥ 2` for all
`p | kw`. For `p ∤ k` this holds because `w` is powerful. For `p | k` it reads
`v_p(k) + v_p(w) ≥ 2`, and requiring it of all three elements is the displayed
condition. ∎

**Lemma 1′ (admissible multipliers of a root). PROVED.**
Let `T₀ = (w₁, w₂, w₃)` be any triple of positive integers and `m ≥ 1`. Then
`m·T₀` is componentwise powerful **iff**

1. every prime `p` with `v_p(w_i) = 1` for some `i` divides `m`, and
2. for every prime `p | m`, `v_p(m) ≥ 2 − min_i v_p(w_i)`.

*Proof.* `m·w_i` is powerful iff for every prime `p | m·w_i`,
`v_p(m) + v_p(w_i) ≥ 2`. If `p ∤ m`, the condition is `v_p(w_i) ∈ {0} ∪ [2,∞)`
for each `i`; a prime with some `v_p(w_i) = 1` therefore forces `p | m`,
giving (1). If `p | m`, then `p | m·w_i` for all `i`, and the condition over
all `i` is exactly `v_p(m) ≥ 2 − min_i v_p(w_i)`, giving (2). Conversely (1)
and (2) cover every prime dividing any `m·w_i`. ∎

Consequences: a perfect-square `k` is admissible for every *powerful* triple;
scaling by an admissible multiplier preserves the AP property but **not**
automatically consecutiveness — a powerful intruder may land inside the scaled
window. Which admissible multiples survive is decided by the census.

**Lemma 2 (two squares force a large gap). PROVED.**
If a consecutive triple `(x, y, z)` with common difference `d` contains two
perfect squares, then `d > √x`.

*Proof.* Say `s² < t²` are two of the elements, so `t ≥ s + 1` and
`t² − s² ∈ {d, 2d}`. Since `s² ≥ x`,
`t² − s² ≥ (s+1)² − s² = 2s + 1 > 2√x`, hence `d > √x` (indeed `d > 2√x`
unless the two squares are the outer pair). ∎

**Corollary.** A consecutive triple with `d ≤ √x` contains at most one
perfect square. Fourteen of the sixteen primitives below 10^19 satisfy
`d < √x` (exceptions: P7, `d/√x ≈ 1.82`, and P14, `≈ 1.34`), so for those
fourteen "at most one square" is a theorem, not an observation.

**Result C3 (squares).** Fifteen of the sixteen primitives contain exactly
one perfect square. **P15**, at 4.15×10^17, is the unique primitive below
10^19 with **no** square element — the first known consecutive triple of this
type. ("At most one" is forced by Lemma 2 for the fourteen primitives with
`d < √x`; "at least one" is simply false in general, as P15 shows.)

**Result C4 (no quadruples).** No four consecutive powerful numbers below
10^19 form an arithmetic progression.

**Result C5 (roots and saturation).** In root coordinates:

- P1's class is generated by **(48, 49, 50)**: the smallest consecutive
  triple is 36·(48, 49, 50). A root with `d₀ = 1` is a scaled AP of
  *consecutive integers*; by Erdős–Mollin–Walsh (open **(secondary)**) the
  root itself should never be powerful, and indeed no root in the census is.
- No chain stays consecutive for every admissible multiplier. The dominant
  chain P8 (272 of the 346 triples; its gap is 0.5% of the local mean powerful
  gap) keeps every admissible multiple below `m = 288` consecutive and loses
  `m = 288` (root-relative; = 72× the primitive) to an intruder. The smallest
  failure anywhere is P1's `m = 72`:
  `2·(1728, 1764, 1800) = (3456, 3528, 3600)` is an AP of powerful numbers
  but the powerful intruder `3481 = 59²` lands inside it.
- One chain *is* saturated in the observed range: P6, root
  (28440875, 28440882, 28440889) with `d₀ = 7` — all 3 admissible
  `m ≤ 200000` occur as consecutive triples.
- P15's chain contains members at ratios 5/2 and 45/2 to its primitive —
  consecutive triples in one class need not be integer multiples of the
  smallest one (they are all integer multiples of the *root*, multipliers
  {1800, 4500, 5400, 9000, 16200, 40500}).
- Every observed multiplier satisfies Lemma 1′ (mechanically re-checked;
  zero exceptions), and every first-missing multiplier is tabulated in
  `data/analysis_1e19.txt`.

**Result C6 (odd differences).** Three primitives (P3, P10, P12) have odd
`d`. The earliest, P3 at 1.49×10^11, is in van Doorn's own range; whether he
remarks on parity we cannot check **(secondary — his table is not readable

## 4. Methodology and verification

Enumeration: every powerful number is uniquely `a²b³` with `b` squarefree;
generate all values below `X` (sieve squarefree `b ≤ X^{1/3}`, loop
`a ≤ √(X/b³)`), sort, scan adjacent gaps for `g_{i+1} = g_i`; segmented
variant for large `X` with a 3-element carry across segment boundaries. All
arithmetic is 64-bit unsigned integer with 128-bit overflow guards; **no
floating point in any critical path** (the "tightness" ratios printed by
`analyze.py` are display cosmetics, not data; 10^19 < 2^64).

Evidence for completeness of the sweep:

1. **Uniqueness self-test**: zero duplicate values among the 6,869,227,848
   generated numbers below 10^19 (a duplicate would mean the `a²b³` generator
   is wrong; uniqueness of the representation is a theorem).
2. **External count anchors**: powerful numbers ≤ 10^10, 10^11, 10^12 =
   214122, 680330, 2158391, matching OEIS A118896 **(secondary)**. The 10^19
   total is within 0.05% of the `ζ(3/2)/ζ(3)·√X` main term (sanity, not
   certification).
3. **Calibration at the published frontier**: exactly van Doorn's 18 triples
   below 10^14, smallest (1728, 1764, 1800), d = 36 **(secondary)**.
4. **Replication**: the 10^18 sweep was run with segment sizes 2×10^16 and
   3700000000000037, and the 10^19 sweep with 2×10^16 and
   33000000000000037 — identical triple lists and totals in both pairs —
   and the monolithic and segmented programs agree at 10^14 and 10^16.
5. **Independent per-triple verification**: `verify_triples.py` re-checks
   every listed triple with a different powerfulness algorithm (trial
   division + square-cofactor test) and re-derives consecutiveness by
   enumerating all powerful numbers in `[x, z]` from scratch. **346/346
   pass.**

Cost (single thread, 4-core/15 GB box): census to 10^18 3m29s / ≤ 2.5 GB
(segmented); to 10^19 7m52s; monolithic 10^17 77 s / 5.6 GB. Verification of
346 triples: 1m51s. Chain analysis: 1.2 s. No randomness anywhere.

## 5. What this says about the conjecture. NUMERICAL.

Raw triple counts are dominated by the multiplier supply of one tight chain
(P8 contributes 272 of 346), so they mostly measure Lemma-1′ arithmetic, not
new events. The informative curve is the **primitive** count: 6 below 10^14,
then 8, 10, 11, 15, 16 at 10^15…10^19. New primitives keep arriving across
the entire observed range — consistent with van Doorn's conjecture, and five
decades more evidence than its published basis — but this is evidence, not
proof, and no growth model is fitted (sixteen points do not constrain one
usefully).

## 6. Open questions

1. Prove any infinite family of consecutive triples (the conjecture). The
   chain structure shows it would suffice to prove that a single root keeps
   infinitely many admissible multiples consecutive — but C5 shows
   consecutiveness fails infinitely often along every chain observed for
   long, so a density argument is needed, not a construction alone.
2. Is P15 the first of infinitely many squareless primitives?
3. Is there a four-term consecutive AP? (None below 10^19.)
4. P1's root (48, 49, 50) has `d₀ = 1`; P6's has `d₀ = 7`. Which integer APs
   with tiny `d₀` and gcd 1 admit *any* powerful multiple — and can `d₀ = 1`
   recur? (For (48, 49, 50) = (2⁴·3, 7², 2·5²), Lemma 1′ makes `m` admissible
   iff `4 | m`, `9 | m`, and any factor of 5 or 7 in `m` appears squared —
   minimal `m = 36`, which is exactly the observed primitive multiplier.
   Every `d₀ = 1` root is three consecutive integers, so EMW-type
   constraints apply to the root itself but not to its multiples.)
5. The first-element sequence 1728, 6912, 729000, … and the primitive
   sequence P1, P2, … appear to be absent from the OEIS **(secondary,
   absence-of-evidence: oeis.org unreachable today; only web-search
   checked)** — verify against the actual OEIS, then submit.
